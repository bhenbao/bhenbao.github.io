RPM Motor Controller

Circuits lab final project · Analog + digital design

For my circuits lab final project, I designed and built a closed-loop speed controller for a DC motor using only discrete ICs and op-amps, with no microcontroller. A dial sets the target speed, an optical sensor measures how fast the motor is actually spinning, and an integrating error amplifier adjusts the motor drive until the two match. Across the dial's range, the motor's speed scaled from about 400 to 4,000 RPM.

How it works

System block diagram: the speed measurement loops back into the error amplifier.

The motor shaft carries a disc with 10 holes, and a phototransistor produces a clock pulse each time a hole passes, so 10 pulses make one revolution. A 5 Hz timing signal defines a 0.2 s window: at the end of each window, a latch pulse saves the counter's value, and a slightly delayed reset pulse clears the counter for the next window. The saved count is proportional to speed, RPM = 30 × count.

An R-2R ladder DAC turns the saved count into a voltage. An op-amp with an R–C feedback network compares that voltage with the setpoint, which comes from a potentiometer, and acts as a proportional-integral (PI) controller: while the motor is too fast the output ramps down, and while it's too slow the output ramps up. A BJT with a flyback diode drives the motor.

Timing: latch and reset pulses

Circuit diagram, delay measurement, and reset pulse.

The counter has to be cleared right after the latch captures its value, so the reset is generated from a delayed copy of the latch signal. A Schmitt-trigger inverter turns the slowly charging RC node (11 kΩ and 1 nF) into a clean edge, and a small differentiator (47 pF and 11 kΩ) turns that edge into a short reset pulse. From the capacitor charging equation and the inverter's threshold voltage, I predicted a delay of 10.8 µs and measured 11.06 µs on the scope.

Counting and converting

Counter, latch, and DAC outputs.

The counter is two cascaded 4-bit counters, giving 8 bits that feed an 8-bit D-latch and the R-2R DAC. I tested each stage on its own before connecting the next. With a 100 Hz counter clock and a 10 Hz latch clock, the latch output stepped in multiples of ten, as expected. The DAC output was a sawtooth that stepped up as the count rose and dropped when the counter reset.

The error amplifier

Circuit diagram, input and output waveforms, and the breadboard build.

I derived the amplifier's output from an ideal op-amp analysis, then tested it with a 5 Hz square wave centred on the 2.5 V setpoint. When the input is above the setpoint, the output ramps down, and when it's below, the output ramps up. That is the behaviour needed to drive the speed error to zero.

Results

Motor speed against setpoint voltage, from the logic analyzer's latch readings (RPM = 30 × count).

The speed rose almost linearly with the setpoint, at roughly 1,600 RPM per volt, from 405 RPM at 0.25 V up to 4,035 RPM at 2.5 V. The top speed is set by the setpoint circuit: the 10 kΩ potentiometer sits in series with a 10 kΩ resistor to 5 V, so the setpoint can't go above 2.5 V.

Debugging

A flat, noisy reset signal. I rewired the circuit, redrew the diagram, and grounded a floating inverter input before finding the real cause: my oscilloscope trigger was set up wrong. Setting the trigger level to 4 V showed the clean pulses that had been there all along.

A counter that stopped at 32. The reset pin on the second counter was tied to the power supply instead of ground. Fixing it restored the full 8-bit count.

A motor that stuttered and had only two speeds. The DAC voltage and the logic analyzer's readings were jumping around, which pointed back to the counter's input. I had misread the inverter's datasheet, with the counter input and the motor clock connected to the wrong sides of the inverter. After rewiring it, the speed responded smoothly to the potentiometer.

What I took away

Two Schmitt-trigger inverters in series clean up an edge without inverting it, which keeps timing signals sharp. An integrating op-amp is a simple way to build feedback that keeps correcting until the error is gone. Testing each stage separately before connecting it to the next made every bug much easier to find.
